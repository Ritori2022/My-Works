# Building Kingdoms in Fantasy Worlds: The Complex Politics of Japanese Isekai Nation-Building

Modern Japanese isekai anime presents a paradox that scholarship is only beginning to unravel. These wildly popular narratives—featuring Japanese protagonists reborn in fantasy worlds who proceed to establish thriving multiracial nations—simultaneously promote messages of peace, cooperation, and diversity while reproducing colonial power dynamics that echo Japan's imperial past. This tension isn't accidental carelessness but rather reflects unresolved contradictions in post-war Japanese identity itself, caught between constitutional pacifism and unprocessed imperial memory, between genuine internationalism and persistent cultural nationalism. The genre serves as a cultural mirror, revealing how a society still negotiating its relationship with empire unconsciously reproduces its logics even while consciously rejecting militarism.

The explosive growth of isekai nation-building narratives since 2010 coincides with renewed debates over Japanese national identity, constitutional revision, and geopolitical anxiety about China's rise. Works like *That Time I Got Reincarnated as a Slime* and *Overlord* have become cultural phenomena, spawning endless merchandise, adaptations, and international fandom. Yet scratch the surface of these ostensibly harmless fantasies and troubling questions emerge: Why do these stories so consistently feature Japanese protagonists "civilizing" primitive fantasy races through superior knowledge? Why does Japanese food, technology, and management philosophy always prove superior to indigenous systems? Why do slavery and racial hierarchies appear so frequently, often without critical examination? The answers lie not in individual creator intent but in deeper cultural currents flowing from Japan's unfinished reckoning with its history as both colonizer and colonized.

## The anatomy of isekai imperialism: How conquest became cozy

The standard isekai nation-building formula follows a remarkably consistent pattern that academic Zachary Gottesman identifies as reflecting "the Japanese settler unconscious." A Japanese protagonist—typically a socially unsuccessful male otaku—dies (often via "Truck-kun") and reincarnates in a medieval fantasy world with special powers or modern knowledge. He encounters a "backward" race (goblins, beastfolk, demons) suffering oppression or poverty. Through superior Japanese knowledge—modern agriculture, hygiene, organizational management, or simply rice and soy sauce—he transforms these primitives into productive citizens. His settlement grows into a village, then town, then nation, attracting diverse races who recognize his enlightened leadership. External threats are decisively defeated through overwhelming power. The protagonist establishes a multiracial utopia where all species cooperate harmoniously under his benevolent rule.

*That Time I Got Reincarnated as a Slime* exemplifies this pattern with disarming gentleness. Corporate worker Satoru Mikami reincarnates as the slime Rimuru and encounters starving goblins and wolves on the brink of extinction. By naming them—a magical act granting power and evolution—Rimuru transforms weak goblins into capable warriors and workers. The series devotes considerable attention to infrastructure: sewage systems, paved roads, housing construction, trade networks. Rimuru explicitly aims to create "a nation that will serve as a bridge between monsters and humans, where monsters and humans both exist and prosper together." The Jura-Tempest Federation emerges as a cosmopolitan success story featuring orcs, ogres, dwarves, and eventually humans coexisting peacefully.

Yet critical observers note disquieting undertones in this benevolent fantasy. Blogger Wis[s]e Words identifies the naming scenes as colonial power dynamics: "The unnamed people gain recognition and value only when named by the colonial authorities. Before that, they're just a mess of small fry." When Rimuru names the goblins and ogres, they physically transform—"the men always become buff slabs of beef while the women gain bouncy bouncy chests"—becoming more human-like, conventionally attractive, and civilized through contact with the Japanese protagonist. This transformation literalizes the civilizing mission: colonial contact doesn't just improve the natives' material conditions but fundamentally elevates their very being. They weren't truly persons before the colonial encounter granted them recognition and form.

The pattern repeats across the genre. *How a Realist Hero Rebuilt the Kingdom* features protagonist Souma Kazuya explicitly applying modern economic and political theory to save a failing medieval kingdom, introducing everything from sewage systems to musical broadcasting. *Dr. Stone* literalizes technological colonialism by having protagonist Senku rebuild scientific civilization from scratch, teaching stone-age humans the wonders of chemistry and engineering. *Farming Life in Another World* presents a gentler version where protagonist Hiraku establishes an idealized rural village that gradually attracts diverse monster races seeking his leadership. Even *Restaurant to Another World* and *Isekai Izakaya*—ostensibly about food—function as cultural imperialism vehicles, depicting fantasy world inhabitants overwhelmed by the obviously superior taste of basic Japanese cuisine, dishes that become so addictive they return religiously for more.

## The Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere's long shadow

Understanding why these narratives feel troublingly familiar requires examining Japan's last major nation-building project: the Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere. Announced in 1940 by Foreign Minister Matsuoka Yōsuke, the GEACPS envisioned a pan-Asian union spanning Japan, China, Southeast Asia, and potentially India and Australia under Japanese leadership. The propaganda was remarkably similar to modern isekai rhetoric: Japan as Asia's moral leader fighting Western colonial exploitation, bringing modernization and prosperity to backward neighbors who lacked the technological sophistication to thrive independently. The slogan *Hakkō ichiu*—"eight corners under one roof"—expressed Japan's divine mission to unify the world under benevolent imperial leadership.

The ideology combined fascist technocratic planning with pan-Asianist liberation rhetoric. Japanese technocrats like Kishi Nobusuke conceived the sphere as a Grossraumwirtschaft where member countries would contribute according to their abilities—raw materials, labor, capital, technology—for collective benefit under Japanese coordination. Official propaganda depicted this as Asian brotherhood: government publications proclaimed Japan's role to "make men of them again" and free Asians from Western oppressors through racial solidarity and Japanese guidance. The 1943 Greater East Asia Conference produced a joint declaration promising cooperation, mutual respect, and shared prosperity.

The brutal reality diverged sharply from propaganda. A 1943 secret document revealed that Japan, as the strongest military power, would "naturally take the superior position" in the hierarchy. The sphere was organized concentrically with Japan at the core, surrounded by lesser spheres of subordinate territories. What the propaganda called liberation meant forced labor, resource extraction, cultural assimilation, and widespread atrocities. Puppet governments had no genuine autonomy. The civilizing mission masked extractive colonialism serving Japan's war machine.

The structural parallels to isekai are striking: a hierarchy with the Japanese protagonist at the apex, subordinate races contributing their unique abilities under centralized leadership, rhetoric of mutual benefit and cooperation masking unequal power relations, the civilizing mission justified by technological and cultural superiority, violence against external enemies presented as defensive and necessary, pan-racial unity that somehow requires Japanese coordination. Scholar Scott Ma identifies how isekai "mediates and assures the protagonist's introduction of western modernity, under the mantle of Japanese custom, back to the West," creating a fantasy where "Japan" becomes "more modern than the West and yet identifiably Japanese"—precisely echoing wartime rhetoric that positioned Japan as simultaneously modern and traditionally Asian, uniquely suited to lead regional development.

The critical question is whether these parallels reflect conscious neo-imperialism or unconscious cultural memory. The evidence suggests the latter. Post-war Japan underwent comprehensive de-militarization and adopted constitutional pacifism through Article 9, which prohibited maintaining traditional military forces. The "Yoshida Doctrine" centered national identity on economic development over military power, peace diplomacy as distinctive characteristic, and alliance with the United States. This created fundamental tension between Japan as atomic bomb victim emphasizing wartime suffering and Japan as imperial aggressor responsible for colonial atrocities—what scholars call the "victim versus aggressor consciousness" divide.

## The unfinished business of historical memory

Japan's relationship with its imperial past remains contested and unresolved in ways that inevitably shape contemporary cultural production. The "textbook wars" beginning in the 1950s revealed deep divisions over how to represent wartime actions, with conservative revisionists seeking to minimize or romanticize Japanese aggression while progressives pushed for honest accounting. Politicians' visits to Yasukuni Shrine—which honors convicted war criminals alongside ordinary soldiers—continue sparking regional tensions. The late 1990s saw formation of the Association to Promote New History Education, which created revisionist textbooks downplaying imperial crimes. In 2015, LDP member Junko Mihara used the phrase *Hakkō ichiu* in the Diet, drawing criticism for insensitivity to victims of Japanese militarism.

Prime Minister Abe Shinzō's tenure (2012-2020) marked a nationalist revival arguing the "Post-war Regime" formed under U.S. occupation prevented Japan from becoming strong and autonomous. Abe promoted "restoring national pride" and constitutional revision to enable more robust military policy, criticizing post-war pacifism and history education for preventing Japanese from being proud of their country. This "Take Back Japan" nationalism provided context for works like *Gate: Where the JSDF Fought*, which academic Daniel Josephy-Hernández analyzed as "propaganda for Shinzō Abe's nationalist government" attempting to "exorcise the shame of Japan's continued reliance on the United States' nuclear umbrella."

*Gate* is the most explicitly nationalist isekai, featuring a dimensional portal opening in Tokyo through which a fantasy army invades. The JSDF establishes a base in the fantasy world, and the series lovingly details their overwhelming technological superiority: attack helicopters versus medieval soldiers, F-15s versus dragons, artillery obliterating fantasy armies with minimal Japanese casualties. The narrative echoes U.S. war on terror imagery while positioning the JSDF as righteous defenders bringing peace and development to a backwards realm. The show was supported by the actual JSDF as a recruitment tool and regularly vilifies critics of Japanese military policy through "hysterical" anti-war female politicians. Josephy-Hernández notes it "remakes iconic scenes from US Vietnam War films into bloody celebrations of killing technology" while demonstrating "the soul-liberating properties of war."

Yet *Gate* is exceptional in its overt nationalism. Most isekai operate through what Fredric Jameson called the "political unconscious"—ideology embedded in narrative structure rather than explicit argument. Gottesman's analysis of *Goblin Slayer* demonstrates how isekai provides "a space where superexploitation and the redistribution of surplus value are buried within a fantasy of non-alienated, non-commodified labor." The isekai frontier allows fantasies of settlement and development without acknowledging the violence inherent in colonialism. Resources become abundant through the protagonist's superior management. Labor becomes joyful cooperation rather than exploitation. Racial hierarchy becomes natural differentiation of talents. Imperial expansion becomes defensive necessity and humanitarian intervention.

## Why slavery became a genre convention, not a bug

No element of isekai provokes more controversy than slavery's ubiquitous presence. *The Rising of the Shield Hero* sparked international debate when protagonist Naofumi purchases the demi-human slave girl Raphtalia rather than freeing her or opposing the institution. The series frames this as pragmatic necessity—Naofumi needs a companion and slavery provides magical compulsion ensuring loyalty—while depicting their relationship as genuinely caring. Naofumi treats Raphtalia kindly, she grows to love him (developing from child to adult woman), and the slave crest becomes symbolic of their bond rather than oppression. Critics saw this as romanticizing slavery through the "benevolent master" trope; defenders argued the series portrays a complex medieval world where abolition isn't immediately feasible.

The pattern repeats across dozens of series: *Mushoku Tensei*, *Black Summoner*, *How a Realist Hero Rebuilt the Kingdom*, *Harem Labyrinth in Another World*, and many others feature protagonists purchasing slaves, usually young attractive women who become loyal companions or harem members. The justification follows a formula: slavery exists in the world, the protagonist cannot immediately abolish it, purchasing slaves "saves" them from worse masters, the protagonist treats them well, the slaves are grateful for this benevolence. The protagonist becomes an enlightened slave-owner rather than an abolitionist.

CBR analyzed the function of slavery in isekai as "the ultimate fruition" of male power fantasy: "said women are incredibly vulnerable before the hero swoops in to save them." The rescue narrative creates romance through monetary transaction and magical compulsion rather than genuine courtship. Slaves occupy "the bottom of the societal totem pole just like the main characters," creating solidarity through shared marginalization—the otaku protagonist and the slave both rejected by mainstream society. Yet this analogy between social rejection in modern Japan and literal enslavement reveals profound ethical blindness.

The prevalence traces partially to market dynamics. Most modern isekai originate from Shōsetsuka ni Narō, a user-generated fiction site where popularity determines professional publication. When *Shield Hero* succeeded with a slave companion, the trope proliferated through imitation. But this economic explanation doesn't address why slavery appeals or why authors and audiences find "benevolent slavery" unproblematic. A Sufficient Velocity forum commenter connected it directly to colonialism: "the Isekai genre at its worst is about poorly disguised author/audience inserts civilising/saving/uplifting/white-man's-burdening less advanced/progressive/safe settings... Is it really a shock that the same people who make and like 'Colonialism, The Genre' want to engage in some slave-play?"

The slavery trope reveals fantasy at its most troubling: the desire for absolute power over grateful subjects who require no persuasion, offer no resistance, provide unconditional loyalty through magical compulsion, and appreciate their subjugation as liberation. This is the colonial fantasy stripped to its essence. The magic crest controlling Raphtalia literalizes the colonial claim that indigenous peoples benefit from their subjugation, that civilization requires hierarchical control, that the colonized should thank their colonizers.

## When the villain is the protagonist: Overlord's ambiguous politics

If most isekai sanitize colonialism through benevolent protagonists, *Overlord* takes a different approach by making the protagonist explicitly evil. Salary worker Suzuki Satoru becomes trapped in the game world Yggdrasil as his undead avatar Ainz Ooal Gown, leader of the Great Tomb of Nazarick filled with NPC servants who view him as an absolute god. Unlike Rimuru's gentle nation-building, Ainz's approach is calculating and brutal: massacring armies, conducting horrific experiments on captives, casually ordering genocide when strategically convenient. The series presents these actions matter-of-factly rather than celebrating them.

A particularly infamous sequence in Season 3 shows Ainz infiltrating an adventurer party and leading them into his tomb as a trap. When captured, the adventurers suffer fates worse than death: the elf girl and her boyfriend become hosts for parasites, the priest becomes an immortal experiment subject perpetually dying and reviving. Fan reactions revealed genuine horror: "I know Ainz isn't necessarily a good person but couldn't he have at least granted everyone a painless death without putting the fear of cthulu in them?" Another stated: "I personally find Overlord to be the Monster equivalent to Hitler and the Nzi party... I'm sick of his condescending attitude and his racist underlings."

Yet *Overlord* functions less as straightforward villainy than as genre deconstruction. Ainz doesn't intentionally pursue evil but stumbles into conquest through misunderstandings. His NPC servants interpret his casual remarks as grand strategic plans and execute atrocities believing they serve his vision. Ainz himself often thinks "Jesus Christ, did we really have to go THAT far?" but cannot show weakness before subordinates who expect an evil overlord. The undead transformation gradually strips his humanity, shown through his emotional suppression ability repeatedly activating when human empathy emerges. His character Pandora's Actor wears a Nazi uniform—which author Maruyama stated he "thought looked cool" during his adolescent phase—an aesthetic choice Ainz now finds "completely embarrassing" but cannot undo.

This self-awareness distinguishes *Overlord* from straightforward power fantasy. The series examines what happens when someone gains absolute power without moral constraints, how evil becomes banal through bureaucratic distance, how subordinates enable atrocity through obedience and interpretive excess. Yet the ambiguity remains troubling: does depicting evil critically differ substantially from depicting it attractively? Does making Ainz sympathetic—showing his internal discomfort while he orders massacres—ultimately humanize fascism? The series' enormous popularity suggests audiences enjoy the power fantasy even when framed as cautionary tale.

The Nazi aesthetic deserves particular attention. Multiple anime incorporate fascist imagery—SS-style uniforms in *ACCA: 13-Territory Inspection Dept.*, military aesthetics in *Attack on Titan*, ultranationalist themes in various works—often framed as "just design" or ironic distance. A forum analysis noted that "ironic racism is still racism; ironic fascism is still fascism." When fascist aesthetics are made palatable through moe characters and disavowed through irony, they become culturally available for unironic appropriation. The fact that actual neo-fascist movements internationally have appropriated anime aesthetics suggests this ironic distance collapses in practice.

## The defense: Why creators say critics are missing the point

Listening to creators themselves complicates the imperialist critique. Fuse, author of *That Time I Got Reincarnated as a Slime*, stated in interviews that he discovered the series' themes only while editing volume 2. He "was making up the plot as he went along" and initially wrote it "purely for the fun of it" without planning nation-building elements. The theme became "Do what you can do for today" and valuing friendships made along the way—not political commentary on governance or colonial power. Fuse revealed he doesn't watch much anime and his inspiration was simply wanting to depict an "ideal boss" character.

Similarly, *Overlord* author Maruyama stated he wrote to "polarize" by creating something extreme even by overpowered protagonist standards, expecting "only a small minority would stick with it." His inspiration came from frustration finding works that appealed to him and from Dungeons & Dragons campaign experiences. Neither creator claims political intent. When *Gate* author Takumi Yanai was asked about political motives, he responded: "No it's just simple entertainment; I never thought about it at all"—though critic Fujita retorted: "What a load of bullshit lol. You can't say it has no political element."

This raises fundamental questions about cultural criticism: Must authors consciously intend political messages for those messages to exist? Can works be imperialist if creators genuinely didn't think about imperialism? The consensus in literary and cultural studies is yes—ideology operates most powerfully when unconscious and naturalized. Jameson's concept of the "political unconscious" describes how narratives function as "socially symbolic acts" encoding cultural anxieties and desires their creators may not recognize. The fact that similar patterns emerge across hundreds of isekai works by different authors suggests these patterns reflect broader cultural currents rather than individual political commitments.

Defenders point to genuine progressive elements in many isekai. Academic analysis in the journal *ImageTexT* reads *Tensei Slime* as expressing "utopian yearnings of hopefulness, progress, and peace as a possible alternative in our increasingly intolerant, xenophobic, and insular world." Rimuru explicitly rejects racial hierarchies, incorporating former enemies as equals. His nation operates through council meetings with representatives from all races in seemingly democratic fashion. The series emphasizes education and mutual understanding over domination, with Rimuru stating: "I want to create an environment where humans can learn. Maybe if they learn more about us, they can be good neighbors, and maybe we can erase the boundary between humans and monsters."

These works also reflect post-war Japanese pacifism rather than militarism. Rimuru is characterized as a "Martial Pacifist" who "often tries to find a peaceful resolution to every conflict but is willing to resort to lethal violence if the situation calls for it." His foreign policy prioritizes diplomacy and mutually beneficial trade over conquest. While extremely powerful and capable of defeating human armies, his goal is cooperation and harmony. This emphasis on peace and international cooperation resonates with Japan's constitutional pacifism and post-war identity as a nation rejecting military solutions.

Some defenders argue that critical readings impose Western postcolonial frameworks inappropriate for Japanese cultural context. These works reflect anxieties specific to contemporary Japan—economic stagnation, social isolation, rigid workplace hierarchies, declining global influence—rather than imperial nostalgia. The protagonist's social failure in modern Japan and success in a fantasy world where effort brings visible results addresses real psychological needs in a society experiencing prolonged economic malaise and social atomization. The isekai boom since 2010 correlates with the "lost decades" following Japan's bubble burst, suggesting these narratives serve therapeutic functions for audiences experiencing precarity and meaninglessness.

## Comparative colonialisms: How Japan, the West, and China fantasize about empire differently

Situating isekai within broader traditions of fantasy literature reveals both universal patterns and culturally specific variations in how different societies imagine power and governance. J.R.R. Tolkien's *Lord of the Rings*—foundational to modern fantasy—presents explicit anarcho-monarchism, with Tolkien advocating for "unconstitutional monarchy" where kings are bound by oath to people and God but not democratic institutions. His ideal ruler is a monarch "whose chief interest in life is stamps, railways, or race-horses" who won't interfere in people's lives. The legendarium features profound racial essentialism with distinct species possessing inherent moral qualities; orcs are irredeemably evil through Morgoth's corruption, creating what critics call "biological determinism... that model is Nazism."

Tolkien explicitly intended to create a "national mythology for England" rooted in Anglo-Saxon and Norse traditions. The racial sequestration praised in his work—distinct ethnic regions seen as more conducive to self-rule than cosmopolitan mixing—reflects conservative cultural nationalism of mid-20th century Britain. Yet Western fantasy after Tolkien increasingly questioned these assumptions: *A Song of Ice and Fire* presents brutal realpolitik where "in the game of thrones, you win or you die," depicting power's corruption and institutional failure rather than celebrating monarchy. Modern American science fiction like Star Trek attempts "benevolent space colonialism" through the Federation that claims to respect sovereignty while continuously seeking to "explore strange new worlds" and "absorb new territory," what critics call "an empire that exists without features of conquest."

Chinese web novels provide instructive comparison. The xuanhuan, xianxia, and wuxia genres emerged from early 20th century Chinese nationalism responding to the "century of humiliation." These works were explicitly "fuelled by a modern Chinese nationalism that focused on anti-imperialism, national unity and national revival," providing "psychological compensation for foreign aggressions, imperialist expansion, warlordism, ineffectual government and economic hardship." The cultivation system—where protagonists systematically progress through power realms toward immortality—reflects Confucian and Daoist cosmology but also enables transparent meritocracy where ability determines status.

Contemporary Chinese web novels explicitly channel "Chinese rise" narratives, with works like *The Three-Body Problem* appealing to cybernationalists because "the theory of the jungle in the novel coincides with the narrative model of Chinese cybernationalism"—"imperialism is determined to destroy us" and "we must rely on strength to speak." These works often feature strong anti-foreign sentiment and nation-building where "the collective well being of a nation and its people as well as the morale of armies" determine power. Unlike Japanese isekai's subtle cultural nationalism, Chinese web novels embrace explicit nationalist themes celebrating Chinese civilization's superiority and historical continuity.

Japanese isekai occupies a middle position: less explicitly nationalist than Chinese web novels but more systematically colonial than post-Tolkien Western fantasy. The use of generic medieval European settings rather than Japanese contexts creates distance that enables what scholar Scott Ma calls "literary Occidentalism"—staging "unequal power relationship between Japan and Europe" where the Japanese protagonist proves modernity and rationality exceed the West's. This represents fascinating reversal of historical power relations: rather than West colonizing East, Japanese protagonist colonizes fantasy-Europe, demonstrating Japanese superiority through knowledge that enables civilizing the backwards West.

All three traditions use fantasy nation-building to process contemporary political anxieties. Western fantasy works through post-imperial guilt, democratic crisis, and search for legitimate authority from a position of declining empire. Chinese web novels channel nationalist resurgence and great power aspirations from a position of rising power reclaiming status. Japanese isekai represents subtle cultural nationalism from a position of economic/cultural influence without military/political dominance—soft power colonialism befitting a nation constitutionally prohibited from maintaining offensive military capability.

## When fans become unwitting critics: The discourse wars

Fan reception reveals the interpretive instability of isekai's politics. Progressive critics identify colonial power dynamics throughout the genre. Blogger GAR GAR Stegosaurus argues "the isekai tale is most commonly the story of the colonist or imperialist... The great white savior! Except, you know, Japanese." Critical fans note how frequently isekai features Japanese protagonists "imposing modernized modes of production on medieval fantasy societies," comparing works to Tarzan and "manifest destiny" narratives. The naming scenes in *Tensei Slime* particularly draw fire for depicting "unnamed people gain[ing] recognition and value only when named by the colonial authorities."

*Shield Hero* sparked the most intense Western controversy, with critics condemning both its false rape accusation opening—seen as opposing #MeToo—and its slavery normalization. ComicBook.com stated the series was "especially controversial, with many seeing it as being at odds with the zeitgeist of the #MeToo movement, if not wholly opposing it." Unwinnable magazine called it guilty of believing "slavery can actually be a good thing, provided you're a good owner." Some critics accused the series of "creating a hotbed for incel culture through its depiction of false rape accusations, slavery, and later on, potential sexual grooming."

Yet these controversies largely didn't register in Japan. *Shield Hero*'s business producer Junichiro Tamura stated in a Reddit AMA: "There have not been any controversies regarding the series in Japan, so it is difficult to say." This cultural divide reveals how what Western progressive audiences find deeply problematic often appears unremarkable to Japanese audiences operating in different cultural contexts with different sensitivities around gender, power, and historical memory.

Korean and Chinese fans react particularly negatively to works like *Gate* due to direct experience with Japanese imperialism. One Chinese commenter raised by WWII veterans provided extensive critique comparing *Gate* to Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere propaganda: "Japan starts off with quite the moral high ground, portraying themselves as liberators by empowering the locals, freeing them from oppression and war... Well, we all know what that led to." They criticized the absence of war's horror: "Where are all the crying mothers and wives and sons and daughters?... Where is the prostitution as local women coddle up to foreign soldiers?"

Defensive fans respond that critics are "reading too much into it," that these are "just fantasy" without political meaning, that medieval settings naturally include slavery without endorsing it. A common refrain holds that creators weren't thinking about politics, so political readings are projection. Japanese internet right-wingers (net-uyo) particularly dismiss Korean and Chinese criticism as jealousy: "These idiots can't tell the difference between fiction and non-fiction" and "They're jealous that we're more popular with foreigners." Some defenders argue slavery's inclusion reflects "historical accuracy" for medieval settings, though critics note actual medieval Europe featured serfdom more prominently than chattel slavery.

Nuanced fans acknowledge both progressive and problematic elements. Japanese critic Iida stated about *Gate*: "I think Gate could have been a work that generated some good debates, whilst also succeeding as entertainment... I wanted to see the show present the question 'What is the JSDF?' to viewers, making them think about it not from the left or the right, but rather front on." Another blogger expressed ambivalence: "As much as I love this type of story on a subjective level and experience a great catharsis through watching it, when I look at it from your typical cool-headed and critical perspective, how can I ignore what goes on?!?!"

The discourse reveals how isekai has become a cultural Rorschach test where viewers' political commitments determine interpretation. The same work can be read as genuine multiculturalism or soft colonialism, pacifist fantasy or imperial nostalgia, harmless escapism or insidious ideology. This interpretive instability itself reveals something significant: these works are politically incoherent precisely because they reflect contradictory impulses in contemporary Japanese culture.

## The unconscious compromises of a colonizer that won't admit it was one

The most sophisticated reading acknowledges isekai as neither pure imperialism nor innocent entertainment but rather as symptomatic of Japan's incomplete historical reckoning. Post-war Japanese identity attempted to construct itself as fundamentally peaceful, internationalist, and anti-militarist while sidestepping serious examination of imperial atrocities and colonial exploitation. The victim consciousness focusing on atomic bombings and wartime suffering competed with but largely eclipsed aggressor consciousness that would require acknowledging systematic violence against Asian neighbors. Constitutional pacifism coexisted with conservative revisionism seeking to restore national pride through historical sanitization.

This produced what might be called "colonialism with Japanese characteristics"—a form of cultural nationalism that maintains assumptions of Japanese superiority while disavowing the violence that historically enforced such hierarchies. Isekai embodies this contradiction perfectly: Japanese protagonists do establish themselves atop hierarchies, do impose Japanese values and systems, do function as civilizing forces, and do benefit from overwhelming superiority over indigenous populations. Yet this domination is presented as mutually beneficial cooperation, violence is defensive rather than aggressive, hierarchies reflect natural differences in ability rather than imposed subjugation, and the civilizing mission brings genuine improvement rather than exploitation.

The genre's explosive popularity suggests it satisfies specific cultural needs. For individual Japanese audiences facing economic precarity, social atomization, and rigid hierarchies offering no advancement, isekai provides fantasy where effort brings visible results, where social status can be earned rather than inherited, where recognition and belonging come through competence rather than conformity. For Japanese culture collectively, isekai allows fantasies of international influence and admiration—the grateful fantasy races appreciating Japanese guidance—without the messy military conquest that brought Japan international condemnation. It's imperialism for a country constitutionally prohibited from having an empire, nationalism for a society deeply ambivalent about nationalism.

Yet the cost of this fantasy is moral. By naturalizing hierarchy, romanticizing benevolent autocracy, normalizing slavery through kind masters, depicting indigenous peoples as requiring guidance from civilized outsiders, and presenting cooperation that just happens to benefit the Japanese protagonist most, isekai reproduces colonial logics while obscuring their violence. The fact that creators don't consciously intend these messages makes them more rather than less ideologically powerful—this is how hegemony operates, through common sense that goes without saying.

The slavery trope crystallizes the problem. In attempting to have slavery present for worldbuilding "realism" while avoiding moral complexity through benevolent masters and grateful slaves, isekai recreates precisely the logic actual slave-owners used to justify chattel slavery: that the enslaved benefit from their bondage, that kind treatment makes the institution acceptable, that the enslaved's gratitude proves the relationship's legitimacy. This isn't conscious pro-slavery advocacy but rather profound ethical thoughtlessness about power's nature—the assumption that hierarchy becomes acceptable when those on top are nice about it.

## Toward a synthesis: Multiple truths about fantasy and power

The most honest assessment must hold multiple perspectives simultaneously. Isekai nation-building narratives genuinely do express utopian desires for cooperation, peace, and community across differences. They genuinely do provide psychological relief for audiences experiencing precarity and meaninglessness in late capitalism's social atomization. They genuinely do emerge from creators pursuing entertainment rather than political agendas. These works are not calculated neo-imperialist propaganda.

Yet they simultaneously embody colonial power fantasies, reproduce assumptions about cultural and technological hierarchies, naturalize domination through benevolent framing, and reflect unprocessed imperial memory. These elements are not authorial mistakes that could be edited out but rather structural features emerging from the genre's core premise: What happens when someone with modern knowledge enters a pre-modern world? The answer is inevitably some form of colonialism because the knowledge differential creates radical power asymmetry, and exercising that power—even benevolently—is colonial praxis.

The comparative analysis reveals isekai is not uniquely problematic. Western fantasy from Tolkien to Star Trek contains similar colonial fantasies. Chinese web novels embrace even more explicit nationalism and civilizational supremacy. All cultures that have experienced or aspired to empire produce fantasies justifying their dominance as natural, beneficial, and desired by subordinated peoples. The human attraction to fantasies of righteous power appears universal.

What makes isekai particularly instructive is how it illuminates the tension between Japan's pacifist self-conception and imperial legacy. A nation that constitutionally renounced war while never fully acknowledging colonial crimes, that experienced both colonization (by the West in the Meiji period) and colonizer status (in Asia from 1895-1945), that maintains cultural nationalism while promoting internationalism, that seeks global influence without military power—this contradictory positioning produces contradictory fantasies. Isekai's incoherence reflects Japan's own cultural incoherence about power, hierarchy, and historical responsibility.

The genre's defenders are partly right: these works aren't monolithically imperialist, do contain progressive elements, and serve important cultural functions beyond reproducing ideology. But critics are also right: the patterns are too consistent, the historical parallels too clear, and the ethical blindnesses too consequential to dismiss as innocent entertainment. The truth is that *Tensei Slime* can be both a genuine celebration of multicultural cooperation *and* a soft-colonial fantasy—these aren't contradictory but complementary readings revealing the work's ideological complexity.

## Conclusions: Fantasy as the political unconscious returns

This analysis cannot and should not conclude with simple moral judgment. Declaring isekai "imperialist" or "harmless" would miss the phenomenon's actual significance as a site where contemporary Japanese society negotiates contradictory commitments, processes historical trauma, and imagines alternative social arrangements. These works matter not because they're propaganda but precisely because they're not—because they reveal through their patterns and repetitions what a culture takes for granted, what desires it harbors, what histories it hasn't processed.

The most important insight is recognizing isekai as symptomatic rather than causal. These works don't create imperialist thinking but rather express and reproduce assumptions already circulating in culture. The problem isn't that Japanese people watch *Tensei Slime* and become imperialists—the problem is that a culture with unresolved imperial history produces popular narratives unconsciously replicating imperial logics. Addressing this requires not censoring isekai but rather fostering the kind of historical reckoning that would make audiences uncomfortable with benevolent autocracy fantasies and civilizing mission narratives.

The genre's international popularity complicates matters further. American fans enjoying isekai aren't supporting Japanese imperialism but rather engaging with universal fantasies about power, competence, and belonging. The specific cultural context of Japanese nation-building matters, but so does the general human attraction to stories where we imagine ourselves exceptional, our knowledge valuable, our leadership beneficial. Isekai succeeds globally because colonialism's basic fantasy—that we could be the enlightened outsider who fixes backwards societies—transcends particular national histories.

Yet this universality doesn't excuse specific expressions. The particular way isekai frames hierarchy, the specific aesthetics it employs, the consistent patterns in what races need civilizing and what knowledge proves superior—these reveal distinctively Japanese cultural anxieties and assumptions. The genre's popularity in China, Korea, and Southeast Asia is particularly complex given Japan's actual imperial history in these regions. When Chinese fans reject *Gate* while embracing *Tensei Slime*, they're making distinctions between overt militarism and soft cultural nationalism that matter even if both contain problematic elements.

The future of isekai likely involves increasing self-awareness and deconstruction. Just as Western fantasy moved from Tolkien's straightforward monarchism to *Game of Thrones*' political cynicism, isekai may evolve toward more critical examination of its own tropes. Works like *I'm the Villainess, So I'm Taming the Final Boss* already subvert slavery conventions by having villains explicitly oppose the institution. *KonoSuba* parodies genre conventions through failure and consequence. *Overlord* examines power's corruption even while remaining ambiguous about endorsement. These developments suggest audiences are becoming more critically engaged with the genre's politics.

Ultimately, isekai nation-building narratives function as Jameson's "political unconscious" made visible—ideology in fantastic form. They reveal a Japan still negotiating post-war identity, still processing imperial history, still balancing pacifist constitution with great power aspirations, still working through the contradiction of being Asia's most developed nation while remaining culturally distinct from the West. These fantasies of building perfect multiracial nations under Japanese leadership express genuine desires for peace and cooperation while reproducing assumptions about hierarchy and civilization that echo imperial history. Both things are true, and the tension between them is precisely what makes isekai culturally significant.

The question isn't whether isekai is "good" or "bad" but rather what these enormously popular fantasies reveal about contemporary values, anxieties, and unfinished historical business. The answer is complex, contradictory, and profoundly human: We long for community while desiring hierarchy. We celebrate cooperation while fantasizing about superiority. We consciously reject colonialism while unconsciously reproducing its logics. We are all, in our own ways, isekai protagonists—certain our intervention would benefit those we encounter, convinced our way is best, unable to recognize the colonial assumption embedded in that very certainty. Understanding isekai requires understanding this about ourselves.
